\chapter{Experimental Setup and Performance Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

\section{Dataset and Model}

We evaluate on the RadioML 2016.10a dataset~\cite{otoole2016rml}, a widely
used benchmark for automatic modulation classification comprising 220{,}000
IQ signal frames spanning 11 modulation classes: eight digital (BPSK, QPSK,
8PSK, QAM16, QAM64, PAM4, CPFSK, GFSK) and three analog (AM-DSB, AM-SSB,
WBFM). Each frame consists of 128 complex-valued samples represented as a
$2 \times 128$ real tensor of in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) components.
The dataset covers SNR values from $-20$\,dB to $+18$\,dB in 2\,dB
increments, yielding 20 SNR levels and 1{,}000 frames per (modulation,
SNR) cell.

We partition the data using stratified sampling that preserves the joint
(modulation, SNR) distribution across splits: 70\% training, 15\%
validation, and 15\% test. All reported accuracies are computed on the
held-out test split.

The classifier under attack is the Adaptive Wavelet Network
(AWN)~\cite{li2023awn}, a pretrained model that achieves 92.6\%
classification accuracy at SNR $\geq 0$\,dB on the clean test set. We use
the publicly available pretrained checkpoint without any adversarial
fine-tuning, so the model represents a realistic deployment target that has
not been hardened against adversarial inputs.

\section{Attack Configurations}

We evaluate five attacks drawn from three families: a single-step
gradient attack (FGSM), an iterative gradient attack (PGD), and
optimization-based attacks (CW-$L_2$, EAD-L1, EAD-EN). All attacks are
implemented via the \texttt{torchattacks}
library~\cite{kim2020torchattacks} using a thin wrapper that maps IQ
signals into the $[0, 1]$ range expected by the library and returns
logits only. The mapping is the per-sample min--max normalization
$\tilde{x} = (x - x_{\min})/(x_{\max} - x_{\min})$ defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}, so that $\varepsilon$ is expressed
relative to each sample's own dynamic range---a natural choice for IQ
signals whose peak-to-peak amplitude varies widely across modulations
and SNR levels. This normalization is used consistently throughout
the evaluation; we do not compare against alternative
normalizations.

\section{Defense Configurations}

We evaluate eight defense configurations: the proposed Adaptive-$K$
and seven baselines (Kalman, Wiener, Savitzky-Golay, Gaussian, FIR, and
an empirical randomized-smoothing baseline) plus the undefended baseline.
The proposed method operates entirely in the frequency domain without
retraining and requires no per-SNR calibration.

Table~\ref{tab:defenses} summarizes the defense parameters. For the five
classical filters we perform a grid search over the parameter space at
each SNR level using the validation set with the CW attack; the
best-performing parameter combination per SNR is stored in a calibration
file (\texttt{calibration\_params.json}) and loaded at test time. CW is
chosen for calibration because it is the strongest attack in our threat
model, and we assume the defender does not know the attack type at
deployment. The same CW-calibrated parameters are therefore reused
unchanged against FGSM, PGD, EAD-L1, and EAD-EN; per-attack calibration
is not assumed available in the deployment setting. Representative
per-SNR calibrated values are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}. The
randomized-smoothing baseline runs the majority-vote rule of
Cohen et al.~\cite{cohen2019certified}: $k = 20$ Gaussian-noised
copies at $\sigma = 0.01$ are pushed through the classifier and the
smoothed prediction is the argmax of accumulated votes. The codebase
provides two interchangeable back-ends for this rule---an in-tree
implementation and a wrapper around IBM ART's
\texttt{PyTorchRandomizedSmoothing}~\cite{art2018}---selectable via
the \texttt{use\_art\_rs} configuration flag. Because we do not
compute the Clopper--Pearson lower bound on the top-class probability
$p_A$ and do not enable the abstain option, the corresponding row in
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the empirical accuracy of the smoothed
classifier rather than a certified $L_2$ radius; obtaining such a
certificate would require hundreds to thousands of noisy forward
passes per sample, which we treat as outside our target latency
budget.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}

We report the following metrics:

\textbf{Per-SNR accuracy:} fraction of test samples correctly classified
at each SNR level $s \in \{0, 2, \ldots, 18\}$\,dB. We focus on non-negative
SNR because at SNR $< 0$\,dB the clean accuracy is below 50\% and
adversarial perturbations are masked by channel noise.

\textbf{Weighted-average accuracy:} the mean per-SNR accuracy weighted by
the number of test samples at each SNR level. Because RML2016.10a is
balanced, this reduces to the simple mean over the 10 SNR levels
$\geq 0$\,dB evaluated.

\textbf{Perturbation-budget curves:} accuracy as a function of attack
strength ($\varepsilon$ for FGSM/PGD; optimization constant $c$ for
CW/EAD) to assess defense robustness across the threat spectrum.

\textbf{Confusion matrices:} per-class classification accuracy before and
after defense at a fixed SNR (18\,dB) to identify which modulation classes
benefit most from the proposed method.

All evaluations use 200 samples per (SNR, modulation) cell from the test
split, yielding 2{,}200 samples per SNR level
(200 samples $\times$ 11 modulation classes).

Figure~\ref{fig:defense_overview} provides a visual overview of
Table~\ref{tab:main}. Four key findings emerge.

\textbf{Finding 1: optimization attacks are devastating without defense.}
CW reduces accuracy from 92.6\% to 35.4\%, EAD-L1 to 6.6\%, and EAD-EN to
3.4\%---near chance level for 11 classes. These norm-minimizing attacks
produce perturbations that are difficult to detect in the time domain yet
catastrophically effective.

\textbf{Finding 2: Adaptive-$K$ achieves the largest recovery.}
On CW, Adaptive-$K$ recovers accuracy from 35.4\% to 71.3\% (+35.9\,pp).
On EAD-L1 the recovery is even larger: from 6.6\% to 70.8\% (+64.2\,pp).
The margin over Kalman (the best classical filter) is 6.2\,pp on CW
(71.3\% vs.\ 65.2\%) and 8.5\,pp on EAD-L1 (70.8\% vs.\ 62.3\%).

\textbf{Finding 3: classical filters are counterproductive on FGSM.}
On FGSM, the undefended baseline (48.1\%) exceeds every classical filter:
Kalman (46.8\%), Gaussian (46.0\%), FIR (46.4\%), Wiener (45.6\%), and
Savitzky-Golay (45.6\%). FGSM perturbations are spectrally diffuse and
resemble the signal itself, so time-domain smoothing removes signal energy
alongside perturbation energy. Adaptive-$K$ achieves 52.0\%, recovering
3.9\,pp above the undefended baseline.

\textbf{Finding 4: empirical randomized smoothing is unsuitable for AMC.}
Even with a full $k = 20$-copy majority vote at $\sigma = 0.01$, the
smoothed classifier collapses accuracy to $\sim 30\%$ across all attacks
(both clean and adversarial), close to the 11-class chance rate. The
additive Gaussian noise destroys the fine-grained IQ structure that AMC
classifiers rely on, so the majority vote is dominated by noise rather
than by the underlying modulation. Because we do not compute the
Clopper--Pearson bound on $p_A$, this row is the empirical accuracy of
the smoothed classifier and should not be interpreted as a certified
$L_2$ radius; obtaining a certificate would require far more forward
passes and would only lower the reported number.

\emph{Low-SNR regime (0--4\,dB).}
At low SNR, the CW attack is weaker because channel noise already degrades
the signal. The SNR-adaptive K-cap of Adaptive-$K$ detects this regime
and reduces filtering aggressiveness, avoiding the accuracy degradation
that fixed-$K$ methods suffer at low SNR.

\emph{Moderate-SNR regime (6--14\,dB).}
The defense advantage is most pronounced here. The attack is fully
effective (undefended accuracy drops well below 40\%) while the signal is
strong enough for meaningful recovery. Adaptive-$K$ maintains a
consistent 6--9\,pp advantage over the best classical filter (Kalman).

\emph{High-SNR regime (16--18\,dB).}
Even at SNR $= 18$\,dB, CW still reduces undefended accuracy substantially,
demonstrating that optimization attacks remain effective at high SNR.
Adaptive-$K$ provides the largest absolute recovery here, as the
stronger signal tolerates more aggressive spectral filtering.

Four patterns emerge from Table~\ref{tab:per_mod}.

\textbf{Undefended accuracy varies widely across modulations.}
QAM16/QAM64/QPSK/8PSK collapse to $\leq 2\%$ under CW, but
BPSK ($40\%$) and CPFSK ($64\%$) remain relatively high even
without any defense. This is expected: BPSK has only two symbols
with maximum inter-symbol distance, and CPFSK is a frequency
modulation whose decision is not moved by small amplitude/phase
perturbations. The recovery gain is smaller on these two rows
because there is less accuracy to recover.

\textbf{Narrowband digital modulations recover strongly.}
QAM64 recovers from 0\% to 90\% and QPSK from 38\% to 86\%; PAM4
($30\to88$\%) and GFSK ($24\to90$\%) follow the same pattern. Their
concentrated spectral profiles are ideal for Top-$K$ filtering.

\textbf{Wideband routing rescues AM-SSB.}
Without wideband routing, Top-$K$ would destroy AM-SSB. The
spectral-flatness routing in Adaptive-$K$ correctly identifies
AM-SSB samples and applies quantization instead, recovering accuracy
to 94\%.

\textbf{AM-DSB benefits from the SNR-adaptive cap.}
AM-DSB recovers to 96\% because its ultra-narrowband spectrum (most
energy in DC) is well-fit by a very small number of FFT bins. The
SNR-adaptive cap preserves this at low SNR as well, whereas a fixed
aggressive cap would over-filter when the signal is weak.

\section{Clean-Signal Degradation}

A practical concern for any input-transformation defense is its effect on
clean (unattacked) signals. On clean signals, Adaptive-$K$ reduces
accuracy by less than 0.5\,pp from the baseline 92.6\%. The SNR-adaptive
K-cap and wideband routing ensure that signal energy is preserved: at
high SNR, nearly all Top-$K$ bins contain signal; at low SNR, the gentler
cap retains more bins; and wideband modulations bypass the filter entirely
via the quantization path. Classical filters cause 1--3\,pp degradation
because their fixed smoothing kernels suppress high-frequency signal
features.

\section{AWN FPGA Inference Latency Estimate}
\label{sec:fpga}

The GPU latency reported above assumes a server-class deployment. To assess
feasibility on embedded receiver-side hardware we additionally evaluate the
AWN classifier itself on an int8 Verilog implementation. Every arithmetic
op of AWN's forward pass is implemented as a synthesizable Verilog module
(\texttt{gemm\_s8}, \texttt{requantize\_s32\_s8}, \texttt{leaky\_relu\_s8},
\texttt{relu\_s8}, \texttt{lut\_s8} for tanh and sigmoid,
\texttt{eltwise\_addsub\_s8}, \texttt{mul\_s8}, \texttt{avgpool1d\_s8}) and
chained by a Python orchestrator into a full int8 inference. Every op-level
module is tested bit-exactly against a numpy fixed-point reference, and the
end-to-end argmax of the int8 hardware path matches the fp32 PyTorch
reference on the RML2016.10a checkpoint.

\subsection{Operator Profile and Cycle Budget}

Profiling the AWN forward pass yields 34 ops that collapse into 9 distinct
int8 hardware primitives after BatchNorm folding (Dropout, padding, concat,
and reshape are absorbed as software data movement). The total multiply-
accumulate (MAC) count is $5.98\,\text{M}$, with the budget concentrated in
convolutions. Two op groups dominate: the second front-end 1D convolution
(\texttt{conv2}) and the four 1D convolutions inside the lifting scheme's
Predictor and Updator chains.

\subsection{Latency vs.\ Clock Frequency and GEMM Parallelism}

The end-to-end FPGA latency is dominated by the GEMM datapath:
\begin{equation}
  T_{\text{infer}} \approx \frac{\text{MACs}_{\text{total}}}
                              {P \cdot f_{\text{clk}}}
    + \frac{\text{cycles}_{\text{non-GEMM}}}{f_{\text{clk}}},
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the GEMM parallelism (number of MACs per cycle) and
$f_{\text{clk}}$ is the system clock. Table~\ref{tab:fpga_latency} shows
how the estimated per-sample inference latency scales jointly with clock
frequency and $P$. With $P = 1$ at 100\,MHz, AWN inference takes
approximately 61.5\,ms per sample; doubling the clock halves the latency,
and increasing $P$ reduces latency near-linearly until the non-GEMM
cycles become the bottleneck.

\subsection{Verification Status}

The AWN hardware pipeline is validated at three levels.
First, each of the 10 distinct op-level Verilog modules passes bit-exact
testbenches against a numpy fixed-point reference that uses the same
$\bigl(x \cdot \text{mul} + \text{half}\bigr) \gg \text{shift}$ saturating
arithmetic as the hardware.
Second, the end-to-end int8 inference produces logits that match the
fp32 PyTorch reference at the argmax level on the RML2016.10a checkpoint.
Third, the cycle-accurate testbench traces give per-op latency that can be
summed to the values in Table~\ref{tab:fpga_latency}, providing a direct
sanity check against the analytical formula above.

\subsection{Implications for End-to-End Deployment}

Combining the Adaptive-$K$ defense (sub-millisecond per batch on GPU,
Table~\ref{tab:latency}) with the AWN classifier on FPGA, the
recovery-plus-classification stack at $P = 64, f_{\text{clk}} = 100$\,MHz
fits within a per-sample budget of roughly $1\,\text{ms}$, which is
compatible with the 1--10\,ms burst processing budget assumed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture} for spectrum-monitoring and ESC-style
receivers. Pushing $P$ to 128 or raising $f_{\text{clk}}$ further (via
pipeline-register insertion in \texttt{gemm\_s8}) brings the AWN inference
itself below half a millisecond, in which case the spectral defense
becomes a free addition relative to the classifier cost.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why a unified defense outperforms separate methods.}
In early prototypes of this work we tried two separate defenses ---
a fixed-$K$ Top-$K$ operator for optimization attacks (CW, EAD) and a
per-sample knee-based Top-$K$ for gradient attacks (FGSM, PGD) ---
because no single fixed rule worked well across attack families.
Adaptive-$K$ replaces both by combining three mechanisms in a single
shared-FFT pass:
(i)~spectral-flatness routing for wideband modulations,
(ii)~SNR-adaptive K-cap to balance aggressiveness with signal
preservation, and
(iii)~per-sample knee estimation to adapt to each modulation's spectral
concentration. The result is a single method that matches or exceeds both
predecessors on every attack type evaluated.

\textbf{Why classical filters fail on gradient attacks.}
On FGSM, all five classical filters reduce accuracy below the undefended
baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}). FGSM perturbations are computed from the
gradient sign, producing perturbations correlated with the signal.
Time-domain smoothing removes signal energy alongside perturbation energy.
In contrast, the frequency-domain Top-$K$ operator selectively retains
the strongest spectral components, preserving signal structure while
discarding lower-energy bins.

\textbf{Why the EAD-L1 recovery gain exceeds CW.}
EAD-L1's $\ell_1$-minimizing penalty produces sparser perturbations than
CW's $\ell_2$ penalty: EAD-L1 achieves only 6.6\% undefended accuracy
vs.\ 35.4\% for CW (a stronger attack at the same budget), yet
Adaptive-$K$'s recovery gain on EAD-L1 is larger (64.2\,pp vs.\
35.9\,pp). This is consistent with the working hypothesis that
$\ell_1$-sparse perturbations concentrate in fewer spectral bins, making
them more amenable to Top-$K$ removal than the more broadly distributed
CW perturbations.

\textbf{Limitation: defense-aware attacks.}
The evaluation in this thesis assumes a defense-unaware attacker
(Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}). A white-box adversary that incorporates
the Top-$K$ operator into the attack optimization could potentially
circumvent the defense by crafting perturbations that survive the Top-$K$
mask. We leave the evaluation of such adaptive defense-aware attacks to
future work; we do not claim formal robustness in that setting.
