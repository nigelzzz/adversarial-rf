\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automatic modulation classification (AMC) is a fundamental capability in
software-defined radio, cognitive radio networks, and spectrum monitoring
systems~\cite{dobre2007survey,hazza2013overview}. By identifying the
modulation format of received signals without prior coordination with the
transmitter, AMC enables dynamic spectrum access, interference management,
and electronic surveillance. Recent deep learning approaches---including
convolutional networks~\cite{otoole2016rml}, CLDNN architectures~\cite{zhang2023cldnn},
and the Adaptive Wavelet Network (AWN)~\cite{li2023awn}---achieve classification
accuracy above 91\% on the standard RML2016.10a benchmark across 11 modulation
classes. However, the same properties that make deep neural network (DNN)
classifiers effective render them vulnerable to adversarial perturbations:
small, carefully crafted modifications to IQ signals can collapse accuracy
to near 0\%~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing,silvaco2023adversarial_rf,lin2020tactics}.

The adversarial threat to AMC systems is qualitatively different from the
image classification setting that spawned most adversarial ML
research~\cite{goodfellow2015fgsm,madry2018pgd}. RF IQ signals have amplitudes
of approximately $\pm$0.02, so even small $L_\infty$ perturbations can constitute
a large fraction of the signal dynamic range. Optimization-based attacks such
as the Carlini--Wagner $L_2$ attack (CW)~\cite{carlini2017towards} and the
Elastic-Net Attack on Deep Neural Networks
(EAD)~\cite{chen2020ead} are particularly effective: they reduce AWN accuracy
from above 90\% to effectively 0\% at SNR 18~dB. Existing defenses for AMC
fall into two categories: adversarial training, which requires retraining the
classifier on attack-generated examples and must be repeated for each new
attack~\cite{madry2018towards,tramer2017ensemble}; and input-transformation
defenses, which preprocess signals before classification and require no model
modification~\cite{guo2018input,xu2018feature}. However, no systematic
comparison has been made between frequency-domain adaptive methods and the
classical signal processing filters---Kalman, Wiener, Savitzky-Golay, Gaussian,
and FIR---that communications engineers would naturally apply as
baselines~\cite{haykin2002adaptive,proakis2006digital}.

We propose an adaptive spectral defense pipeline that operates entirely in the
frequency domain. Our key insight is that adversarial perturbations on IQ signals
tend to occupy the high-frequency spectral components of the signal rather than
the low-frequency components that carry modulation information. By applying a
fast Fourier transform (FFT) and retaining only the $K$ largest magnitude bins,
we filter out adversarial energy while preserving the underlying modulation
signature. We augment this with a spectral flatness routing criterion that
detects wideband modulations (AM-SSB) where Top-$K$ filtering would destroy
signal content, and an adaptive-$K$ mechanism that automatically selects
per-sample filtering intensity based on the spectral magnitude knee-point.
We benchmark all proposed methods against five calibrated classical signal
processing filters and randomized smoothing~\cite{cohen2019certified}, providing
the first systematic evaluation of this design space for AMC defense.

This paper makes four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Control-plane attack characterization:} We experimentally
  confirm that adversarial attacks on AMC are \emph{control-plane attacks}---
  they cause the classifier to select the wrong demodulator without corrupting
  the underlying signal. Across five attack algorithms (CW, EADL1, EADEN,
  FGSM, PGD) and eight digital modulations, CRC integrity remains above 71\%
  under oracle demodulation at SNR 18~dB, demonstrating that the adversarial
  perturbation leaves the data waveform intact. This motivates frequency-domain
  defense: if the perturbation is a spectral artifact, filtering can remove it.

\item \textbf{Adaptive-$K$ FFT defense:} We propose automatic per-sample
  spectral filtering intensity selection via a magnitude knee-point detection
  algorithm. Rather than using a fixed $K$ for all signals, the adaptive-$K$
  method sorts FFT magnitudes in descending order and finds the elbow where
  cumulative energy exceeds a threshold, setting $K$ equal to the corresponding
  index. This adapts filtering to signal bandwidth, preserving narrow-band
  modulations (BPSK: small $K$) while allowing wider-band signals the
  frequency support they require.

\item \textbf{Systematic classical filter comparison:} We provide the first
  evaluation of five calibrated classical signal processing filters---Kalman,
  Wiener, Savitzky-Golay, Gaussian, and FIR---as adversarial defenses for AMC.
  Filter parameters are calibrated per-SNR via grid search on validation
  accuracy. Our adaptive spectral methods outperform all classical baselines
  by 4--8\% on optimization-based attacks (CW, EAD) at high SNR, while classical
  filters show competitive performance at low SNR where adversarial attacks are
  less effective than channel noise.

\item \textbf{Spectral-gated routing:} We propose a flatness-based bypass
  criterion that routes wideband modulations (AM-SSB, spectral flatness
  $\sim$0.55) to input quantization while routing narrowband modulations
  (spectral flatness $<$0.04) to FFT Top-$K$ filtering. The routing requires
  only one shared FFT computation, adding negligible latency. The gated
  defense recovers AM-SSB from 0\% to 20--65\% accuracy under CW and EAD
  attacks, where Top-$K$ alone achieves 0\%.
\end{enumerate}
