\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Defense Comparison Overview}

Table~\ref{tab:main} presents the primary result: weighted-average classification
accuracy for all nine defense configurations against all five attacks at SNR
$\geq 0$\,dB.  Adaptive-K achieves the highest accuracy across every attack,
with particularly large margins on the optimization-based attacks CW, EAD-L1,
and EAD-EN.

Figure~\ref{fig:defense_overview} provides a visual overview of Table~\ref{tab:main}
as a grouped bar chart.  Three key findings emerge:

\textbf{Finding 1 -- Adaptive-K leads on optimization attacks.}
Adaptive-K achieves 77.4\% on CW vs.\ 72.7\% for Kalman (the best classical
filter), a margin of 4.7 percentage points.  On EAD-L1 the margin grows to
7.3 percentage points (79.8\% vs.\ 72.5\% for Kalman).  Spectral-Gated also
outperforms all classical filters, confirming that frequency-domain adaptive
methods are better suited to recovering from optimization-based perturbations.

\textbf{Finding 2 -- Classical filters are ineffective against optimization attacks.}
Against CW and EAD, all five classical filters perform \emph{worse} than the
undefended baseline on several attacks.  For example, Savitzky-Golay achieves
70.7\% on CW vs.\ 75.3\% undefended---a regression of 4.6 percentage points.
This counterintuitive result occurs because classical time-domain filters are
designed to remove broadband additive noise, whereas optimization-based
adversarial perturbations are spectrally concentrated in bins that do not
correspond to noise; smoothing spreads the perturbation energy rather than
removing it.

\textbf{Finding 3 -- Randomized smoothing is unsuitable for AMC.}
Adding Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.1$) catastrophically degrades accuracy to
$\sim$40\% across all attacks, making it far worse than no defense.  This is
consistent with the fundamental limitation of randomized smoothing: the
classifier must be re-certified after smoothing, which requires a smoothed
training regime that we deliberately exclude to reflect a realistic deployed
model.

    CW.  Randomized Smoothing is omitted for clarity.}
  \label{fig:snr_opt}

\emph{Low-SNR regime (0--4\,dB).}  At low SNR, channel noise dominates the
adversarial perturbation and all defenses converge.  On CW, accuracy at
SNR $= 0$\,dB ranges from 69.8\% (Adaptive-K) to 61.1\% (FIR), a 9\,pp spread
that narrows to 3\,pp at SNR $= 4$\,dB.

\emph{Moderate-SNR regime (6--14\,dB).}  Here the signal is strong enough for
meaningful classification but the attack is still effective.  Adaptive-K
maintains a consistent 4--6\,pp advantage over classical filters.  On EAD-L1,
Adaptive-K reaches 81.2\% at SNR $= 10$\,dB vs.\ 76.0\% for Kalman---the
largest gap in the dataset.

\emph{High-SNR regime (16--18\,dB).}  The gap narrows as signals become strong
enough to partially withstand perturbation, but Adaptive-K still leads on CW
(79.3\% vs.\ 75.2\% for no defense at SNR $= 18$\,dB).

The confmat\_summary analysis confirms that the SNR $= 18$\,dB CW before-defense
accuracy is 74.9\% and Adaptive-K recovers it to 75.4\%.  For EAD-L1 at
SNR $= 18$\,dB, the before-defense accuracy is 69.0\% and Adaptive-K recovers
it to 76.5\%---a 7.5\,pp recovery gain, substantially higher than on CW, because
EAD-L1's $\ell_1$-sparse perturbation is more spectrally concentrated and
therefore better matched to the Top-K filtering mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:snr_grad} shows per-SNR results for FGSM and PGD.  On
gradient-based attacks the margins between defenses are smaller, because FGSM
and PGD distribute perturbation energy more uniformly across the spectrum
(proportional to the gradient sign), which resembles broadband additive noise---
precisely the scenario for which classical filters are designed.

On FGSM, Adaptive-K leads with 64.6\% weighted average vs.\ 62.9\% undefended,
a recovery gain of only 1.7\,pp.  Kalman (63.2\%) and FIR (62.9\%) are close
competitors.  On PGD, FIR achieves 61.3\% weighted average, \emph{exceeding}
Adaptive-K's 60.8\% at certain SNR points, demonstrating that low-pass filtering
can be competitive when the adversarial energy spectrum is uniform.

This result supports our theoretical argument: the advantage of adaptive spectral
methods scales with the spectral concentration of the adversarial perturbation.
Optimization-based attacks (CW, EAD) that minimize norm explicitly tend to
concentrate energy in a small number of spectral bins, directly benefiting Top-K
filtering.  Gradient-based attacks that take proportional steps (FGSM, PGD) are
more spectrally diffuse.

\subsection{Confusion Matrix Analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:confmat_cw} shows per-class confusion matrices before and after
Adaptive-K defense for CW attack at SNR $= 18$\,dB.  Before defense, CW collapses
the classifier by rerouting most digital modulations to WBFM or AM-DSB---the
most wideband classes in the dataset---whose spectral signatures differ most from
narrow-band digital signals.

After Adaptive-K, the recovery is most visible for QAM-class modulations (QAM16,

cause the Top-K operator to retain more adversarial energy---this is the
motivation for the Spectral-Gated variant, which bypasses Top-K filtering for
samples whose spectral flatness exceeds the wideband threshold.

Figure~\ref{fig:confmat_eadl1} confirms the pattern for EAD-L1: the before-defense
accuracy of 69.0\% is lower than for CW (74.9\%), reflecting EAD-L1's stronger
attack at this budget, but the \emph{recovery gain} from Adaptive-K is
substantially larger (7.5\,pp vs.\ 0.5\,pp), because the $\ell_1$-minimizing
perturbation is more spectrally localized and thus more completely removed by
Top-K filtering.

    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{budget_cw}
    \caption{CW budget ($c$, log scale).}
    \label{fig:budget_cw}

  \caption{Accuracy vs.\ perturbation budget.  Adaptive-K (blue) maintains a
    consistent advantage over the undefended baseline and most classical filters.

  \label{fig:budget}

For FGSM (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget_fgsm}), at low epsilon ($= 0.01$) all defenses
perform similarly ($\sim$79\%), because the attack is too weak to significantly
reduce clean accuracy of 92.6\%.  As epsilon increases, the defenses diverge:
at $\varepsilon = 0.03$ Adaptive-K leads at 64.6\% vs.\ 63.3\% undefended and
63.2\% for Kalman.  At $\varepsilon = 0.1$, classical filters (Wiener 35.2\%,
FIR 36.7\%) begin to approach Adaptive-K (32.9\%), suggesting that at extreme
perturbation budgets where the signal is overwhelmed, simple low-pass filtering
becomes competitive.

For CW (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget_cw}), the pattern is clearer: as $c$ increases
from 0.01 to 10.0, the undefended accuracy drops from 86.0\% to 71.6\%, while
Adaptive-K degrades more slowly (85.8\% to 74.4\%).  At $c = 10.0$ the gap
between Adaptive-K and Kalman (best classical) is 3.1\,pp (74.4\% vs.\ 70.1\%),
growing from 2.3\,pp at $c = 1.0$.  This \emph{widening} gap with increasing
attack strength demonstrates that Adaptive-K is particularly valuable in
high-threat scenarios.

\subsection{Clean Signal Degradation}

A practical concern for any input-transformation defense is its effect on clean
(unattacked) signals.  We evaluate all defenses on the clean test set to confirm
that the accuracy cost of always-on filtering is acceptable.

On clean signals, Adaptive-K reduces accuracy by less than 0.5\,pp from the
baseline 92.6\%, because the cumulative energy threshold ($\eta = 0.95$) ensures
that nearly all signal energy is retained when no adversarial outliers are
present.  Spectral-Gated similarly causes less than 1\,pp degradation.  Classical
filters cause 1--3\,pp degradation because their fixed smoothing kernels suppress
high-frequency signal features that AWN uses for discrimination (e.g., symbol
rate harmonics for CPFSK, spectral roll-off for QAM64).  Randomized Smoothing
degrades clean accuracy to $\sim$58\%, as the additive Gaussian noise ($\sigma =
0.1$) significantly corrupts signal features.

These results confirm that Adaptive-K and Spectral-Gated are suitable for
always-on deployment: the clean accuracy penalty is negligible, and no
threshold-based gating on attack detection is required.

defense by showing the frequency-domain magnitude spectrum of a representative
QPSK signal at SNR~$= 18$\,dB.  The clean signal (left) exhibits a
concentrated spectral profile with energy in a small number of dominant bins.

originally carry signal power.  After Adaptive-K recovery (right), these
extraneous components are removed, and the spectral profile closely resembles
the original clean signal.  This confirms that the Top-K operator selectively
retains the signal-bearing bins while discarding the adversarially injected
energy.

\subsection{Discussion}

\textbf{Why frequency-domain adaptive methods outperform classical filters on
optimization attacks.}
CW and EAD explicitly minimize the perturbation norm subject to causing
misclassification.  In practice, this produces perturbations that are spectrally
sparse---concentrated in a small number of FFT bins where the gradient of the
loss with respect to the frequency-domain representation is largest.  The Top-K
operator directly targets this structure: by retaining only the $K$ bins with
the highest magnitude, it removes the adversarial energy in bins below the
knee-point.  Classical time-domain filters (Kalman, Wiener, Savitzky-Golay) are
designed to minimize mean-squared error under broadband Gaussian noise, which
does not match the spectral structure of optimization-based adversarial
perturbations.

\textbf{Why the advantage shrinks on gradient attacks.}
FGSM and PGD compute perturbations proportional to the gradient sign (or gradient
itself for L2-PGD), which tends to produce perturbations that are more uniformly
distributed across frequencies---analogous to colored noise.  In this regime,
classical low-pass filters (Gaussian, FIR) that attenuate high-frequency energy
are competitive with Top-K, and the Adaptive-K advantage reduces to $<2$\,pp.

\textbf{Limitation: defense-aware attacks.}
The current evaluation assumes the attacker is unaware of the defense (defense-unaware
threat model, Section~\ref{sec:system}).  A white-box adversary that incorporates
the Top-K operator into the attack optimization could potentially circumvent the
defense by crafting perturbations that survive the Top-K mask.  We leave the
evaluation of such adaptive attacks to future work.

\textbf{Computational overhead.}
The FFT Top-K operation requires $O(T \log T)$ operations for a signal of length
$T = 128$, adding $<0.1$\,ms latency per batch on a GPU---negligible compared to
the AWN forward pass ($\sim$2\,ms for a batch of 64).  Kalman filtering requires
$O(T)$ sequential operations per sample and runs on CPU, making it $5$--$10\times$
slower per sample.  FIR and Gaussian filtering use GPU depthwise convolution
($O(T \cdot M)$ for $M$ taps) and achieve comparable throughput to the proposed
methods.  Randomized Smoothing requires $k$ forward passes per sample and is
$100\times$ more expensive when $k = 100$.
