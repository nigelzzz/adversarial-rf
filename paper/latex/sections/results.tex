\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Defense Comparison Overview}

Table~\ref{tab:main} presents the primary result: weighted-average classification
accuracy for nine defense configurations against all five attacks at SNR
$\geq 0$\,dB.  The proposed Adaptive-K v2 achieves the highest accuracy on four
of five attacks, with particularly large margins on the optimization-based
attacks CW, EAD-L1, and EAD-EN.

Figure~\ref{fig:defense_overview} provides a visual overview of Table~\ref{tab:main}
as a grouped bar chart.  Four key findings emerge:

\textbf{Finding 1 --- Optimization attacks are devastating without defense.}
CW reduces accuracy from 92.6\% to 35.4\%, EAD-L1 to 6.6\%, and EAD-EN to
3.4\%---near chance level for 11 classes.  These norm-minimizing attacks produce
perturbations that are imperceptible in the time domain yet catastrophically
effective.

\textbf{Finding 2 --- Adaptive-K v2 achieves the largest recovery.}
On CW, Adaptive-K v2 recovers accuracy from 35.4\% to 71.3\% (+35.9\,pp).
On EAD-L1 the recovery is even larger: from 6.6\% to 70.8\% (+64.2\,pp).
The margin over Kalman (best classical filter) is 6.2\,pp on CW
(71.3\% vs.\ 65.2\%) and 8.5\,pp on EAD-L1 (70.8\% vs.\ 62.3\%).
This demonstrates that the unified adaptive spectral approach far
outperforms calibrated classical filters.

\textbf{Finding 3 --- Classical filters are counterproductive on FGSM.}
On FGSM, the undefended baseline (48.1\%) exceeds every classical filter:
Kalman (46.8\%), Gaussian (46.0\%), FIR (46.4\%), Wiener (45.6\%),
Savitzky-Golay (45.6\%).  This counterintuitive result occurs because FGSM
perturbations are spectrally diffuse---resembling the signal itself---so
time-domain smoothing removes signal energy alongside perturbation energy.
Adaptive-K v2 achieves 52.0\%, recovering 3.9\,pp above the baseline.

\textbf{Finding 4 --- Randomized smoothing is unsuitable for AMC.}
Adding Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.01$) collapses accuracy to $\sim$30\%
across all attacks.  The additive noise destroys the fine-grained IQ structure
that AMC classifiers rely on.

\emph{Low-SNR regime (0--4\,dB).}  At low SNR, the CW attack is weaker
because channel noise already degrades the signal.  Adaptive-K v2's
SNR-adaptive K cap detects this regime and reduces filtering aggressiveness,
avoiding the accuracy degradation that fixed-K methods suffer at low SNR.

\emph{Moderate-SNR regime (6--14\,dB).}  The defense advantage is most
pronounced here.  The attack is fully effective (undefended accuracy drops
well below 40\%) while the signal is strong enough for meaningful recovery.
Adaptive-K v2 maintains a consistent 6--9\,pp advantage over the best
classical filter (Kalman).

\emph{High-SNR regime (16--18\,dB).}  Even at SNR $= 18$\,dB, CW still
reduces undefended accuracy substantially, demonstrating that optimization
attacks remain effective at high SNR.  Adaptive-K v2 provides the largest
absolute recovery here, as the strong signal tolerates aggressive spectral
filtering.

On FGSM, Adaptive-K v2 achieves 52.0\% weighted average vs.\ 48.1\%

On PGD, Adaptive-K v2 achieves 43.4\% vs.\ 32.6\% undefended (+10.9\,pp),
the largest margin among gradient attacks.  FIR is the strongest classical
competitor at 38.8\%, still 4.6\,pp below Adaptive-K v2.

Three patterns emerge from Table~\ref{tab:per_mod}:

\textbf{Narrowband digital modulations recover strongly.}
QAM64 recovers from 0\% to 90\% under both normalizations---the highest
per-class recovery.  Its concentrated spectral profile is ideal for Top-K
filtering.  QPSK (0\% $\to$ 76--86\%), PAM4 (6\% $\to$ 82--88\%), and
GFSK (0\% $\to$ 84--90\%) follow the same pattern.

\textbf{Wideband routing rescues AM-SSB.}
Without wideband routing, Top-K destroys AM-SSB (accuracy stays at 48\%
with uncapped Adaptive-K).  The spectral flatness routing in Adaptive-K v2
correctly identifies AM-SSB samples and applies quantization instead,
recovering to 94\% under both normalizations.

\textbf{AM-DSB benefits from SNR-adaptive cap.}
AM-DSB recovers to 98\% (paper norm) because its ultra-narrowband spectrum
(most energy in DC) needs only 1--2 FFT bins.  The SNR-adaptive cap
preserves this even at low SNR, whereas a fixed aggressive cap can
over-filter at low signal levels.

valuable in high-threat scenarios.

significantly affect classification.  At high epsilon ($= 0.3$), all methods
converge near 22\% as the signal is overwhelmed.  Adaptive-K v2 maintains a
small but consistent advantage in the moderate range ($\varepsilon = 0.03$--$0.1$).

\subsection{Clean Signal Degradation}

A practical concern for any input-transformation defense is its effect on clean
(unattacked) signals.  On clean signals, Adaptive-K v2 reduces accuracy by
less than 0.5\,pp from the baseline 92.6\%.  The SNR-adaptive K cap and
wideband routing ensure that signal energy is preserved: at high SNR,
nearly all Top-K bins contain signal; at low SNR, the gentler cap retains
more bins; and wideband modulations bypass the filter entirely via the
quantization path.  Classical filters cause 1--3\,pp degradation because
their fixed smoothing kernels suppress high-frequency signal features.

\subsection{Discussion}

\textbf{Why a unified defense outperforms separate methods.}
Earlier versions of this work used two separate defenses: a fixed Top-K
(Spectral-Gated) for optimization attacks and a per-sample knee-based method
(Adaptive-K) for gradient attacks.  Adaptive-K v2 unifies these by combining
three mechanisms in a single shared-FFT pass: (1)~spectral flatness routing
for wideband modulations, (2)~SNR-adaptive K cap to balance aggressiveness
with signal preservation, and (3)~per-sample knee estimation to adapt to
each modulation's spectral width.  The result is a single method that
matches or exceeds both predecessors on every attack type.

\textbf{Why classical filters fail on gradient attacks.}
On FGSM, all five classical filters reduce accuracy below the undefended
baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}).  FGSM perturbations are computed from the
gradient sign, producing perturbations correlated with the signal.
Time-domain smoothing removes signal energy alongside perturbation energy.
In contrast, the frequency-domain Top-K selectively retains the strongest
spectral components, preserving signal structure while discarding
lower-energy perturbation bins.

\textbf{Why the EAD recovery gain exceeds CW.}
EAD-L1's $\ell_1$-minimizing penalty produces sparser perturbations than
CW's $\ell_2$ penalty: EAD-L1 achieves only 6.6\% undefended accuracy vs.\
35.4\% for CW (stronger attack at the same budget), yet Adaptive-K v2's
recovery gain is larger (64.2\,pp vs.\ 35.9\,pp).  This is because
$\ell_1$-sparse perturbations are more spectrally concentrated, making them
more amenable to Top-K removal.

\textbf{Limitation: defense-aware attacks.}
The current evaluation assumes the attacker is unaware of the defense.
A white-box adversary that incorporates the Top-K operator into the attack
optimization could potentially circumvent the defense by crafting perturbations
that survive the Top-K mask.  We leave the evaluation of such adaptive attacks
to future work.

\textbf{Computational overhead.}
Adaptive-K v2 requires one shared FFT for flatness computation, SNR estimation,
knee detection, and Top-K filtering, plus one IFFT---a total of
$O(T \log T)$ operations for $T = 128$.  This adds $<0.1$\,ms latency per
batch on a GPU, negligible compared to the AWN forward pass ($\sim$2\,ms for
a batch of 64).  Kalman filtering runs on CPU with $O(T)$ sequential operations
per sample, making it $5$--$10\times$ slower.
